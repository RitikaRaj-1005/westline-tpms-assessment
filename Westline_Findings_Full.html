What is happening with the tyres: the short answers

The "nothing but alarms" on the Goa truck

The afternoon drops are the whole truck cooling, not tyres leaking. In all 13 Goa events every one of the 14 tyres lost pressure together, at 0.66 to 0.73 PSI per °C as tyre temperature fell 12 to 18 °C, and pressure largely came back as the tyres warmed. They start on 9 June, which fits Ramesh's afternoon rain, but there is no weather data to prove it, and the same pattern appears on all six trucks. Whether these set off TyreSense alarms needs the alert log. F17

Are the Hubballi roads eating the tyres?

Not mainly. Hubballi tops up in the hot afternoon to the cold card value, so its trucks start every morning 10 to 12 PSI under spec; Belagavi adjusts early in the morning, at much lower tyre temperatures, and starts on spec. Between fills Hubballi tyres lose only 1 to 2 PSI per 30 days more than Belagavi's, far too little to explain the gap, though a small road effect is not ruled out. F14–F16

Could the Karwar failure on 11 July have been foreseen?

Not a day ahead: the tyre was at spec the night before and no earlier day showed any warning. On the day, yes: it pulled away from its twin from about 03:15, passed 10 PSI at 03:40 and 20 PSI at 06:10, well before it fell below 70 PSI at 09:03 and before the truck stopped in Hubballi. Whether anyone could have acted depends on who sees alerts. F12

One alarm, on one tyre

A twin comparison (inner against outer on the same hub) is a candidate: a 10 PSI difference sustained 20 minutes to inspect, 20 PSI as urgent, with a reset band. Over six weeks it would have flagged two real conditions and nothing else, and it stays silent through the afternoon cooling. It is tested on one failure only and does not cover the single tyres on axles 1 to 3. F13

Worth acting on now: fill tyres cold at both depots (or use a hot-fill allowance); inspect Goa 4LI, Karwar 3LO, Gadag 5RO and City 5RI, which lose air several times faster than the spec's 3 PSI a month (F16); replace the Shivamogga 2RO sensor, at 1% battery (F18); fix the data issues before the next export: a duplicated 12-hour overlap, a wrong registration on the Goa truck's July file and the mislabelled C/D side codes (F1–F3).

Assessment overview

Westline Carriers runs six five-axle, fourteen-tyre trucks from two depots, Belagavi and Hubballi. In April 2026 they fitted a TyreSense TPMS on every truck: one sensor per valve stem reporting pressure, temperature and battery, plus a GPS fix. I have six weeks of exports (1 June to 15 July 2026), the vehicle and tyre register with tread-depth inspections, the daily kilometre log, the inflation spec sheet and one transcript of a conversation with the fleet manager, Ramesh Vijayan.

The brief is a single sentence: find out what is happening with this fleet's tyres. Behind it I read three concrete questions from the client:

  • The alarms. The MD says the system produces nothing but alarms. Ramesh describes all fourteen tyres on the Goa truck alarming every afternoon in June and reading normal the next morning.
  • One tyre, one day ahead. Ramesh wants a warning that names one tyre a day before it lets him down, instead of fourteen alarms.
  • The Karwar failure. An inner tyre on the Karwar truck failed on the road in July with no warning anyone knew of. The MD wants to know whether it could have been foreseen.

Ramesh also asked directly whether the Hubballi roads are eating his tyres. I am treating that as a fourth question.

The assessment rewards conclusions that are true, reasoning from how tyres physically behave before reaching for statistics, cross-checking sources against each other, and saying clearly what the pack cannot answer. My plan is to establish a few findings carefully rather than many quickly. My priority order is: data quality first (so nothing later is built on a bad table), then the alarms, the Hubballi pressures and the Karwar failure, then cold pressure against spec, slow leaks and twin-pair balance.

Deliverables: a short deck of the most critical findings (Claude Slides), an HTML file with all relevant findings, five ranked questions for Ramesh, a one-page distrust memo, every AI transcript in full, and all code.

Tyre positions and codes

TPMS codes give axle, side and position: 4LI is the inner tyre on the left of axle 4. Axles 1 to 3 carry single tyres (1 is steer), axles 4 and 5 carry twins. The inspector, Rajan, uses workshop codes: C or D for side, F1 to F3 for axles 1 to 3, R1 and R2 for axles 4 and 5, then I or O. From finding F3, C is the left side and D is the right side, so CF1 = 1LO, DF1 = 1RO, CR1I = 4LI and DR2I = 5RI.

Inflation standard

Cold pressures from the spec sheet, the same for all six trucks: axle 1 at 115 PSI, axles 2 and 3 at 105 PSI, axles 4 and 5 at 100 PSI. These match the card on the depot wall as Ramesh describes it. The spec notes that matter for the analysis:

  • "Cold" means before the truck has run, or after it has stood for at least three hours. A running tyre reads higher.
  • Both tyres of a twin pair must be at the same pressure.
  • Normal loss through the casing is up to about 3 PSI a month. Faster loss points to a puncture, valve, bead or casing problem.
  • Under-inflation of 10 to 15 per cent cannot be seen by eye.

Methodology

Tools. Google Colab with pandas, reading the files directly from my Google Drive folder Westline_Working. Notebook 01_load_and_clean holds the loading and data-quality work. Later notebooks will hold the analysis.

Order of work. I check the data before I analyse it, and I explain what I see from tyre physics first: pressure rises with temperature, so a reading only means something once I know whether the tyre was hot or cold. I only use a statistical method once the physical explanation has been ruled in or out. Where two sources disagree I record it and check against a third source before choosing one.

Cleaning rules applied so far:

  • Truck identity is taken from the file name, not from the Vehicle column (see F2).
  • Rows duplicated across the June and July exports are dropped, keeping one copy (see F1).
  • Only the fourteen sensor slots for axles 1 to 5 are kept; axles 6 and 7 are empty on every truck (see F7).
  • Inspection positions are converted to TPMS codes with C = left, D = right (see F3), and tyres are tracked by serial so the 28 June swap does not mix up two tyres.

Cold readings (Section 5): a rest is a run of consecutive slow-mode records lasting at least three hours with under 200 m of cumulative GPS movement; the cold reading is each tyre's median over the last 10 minutes of the rest, just before the truck moves; morning cold readings are rests ending between 02:00 and 10:00 (median tyre temperature 24.8 °C). Slow mode alone does not make a reading cold (F10). Detected pressure-adjustment events are steps of 3 PSI or more in a tyre's 10-minute median while parked, with temperature changing by less than 1.5 °C; they are detected in the data, not confirmed inflations.

Hypotheses

Each hypothesis is something I expect to be true from the pack but have not yet shown in the data. When one is tested, its status points to the finding that resolves it.

H1. The Goa truck's afternoon alarms reflect a pressure change affecting all fourteen tyres at once, not air loss from individual tyres. Source: transcript (afternoon monsoon on the Goa side, all fourteen alarming together, normal next morning). Test: Section 6. Status: supported with limits (F17). All 14 tyres fell together in every qualifying comparison of all 13 Goa events, at 0.66 to 0.73 PSI per °C as tyre temperature fell, between 13:00 and 17:00 and only from 9 June; pressure largely recovered as temperature returned (9 of 13 events measurable). Rain cooling the tyres is plausible and consistent with Ramesh's account, but not proven: the export has no weather data, missing GPS during events is an association only, and the valve-stem sensor measures air temperature, not the tread. The same pattern appears on all six trucks, including the Karwar truck while parked, so it is not specific to the Goa road. Whether these events set off TyreSense alarms cannot be checked without the alert log.

H2. The Hubballi trucks read low in the morning because air is adjusted while the tyres are hot, not because the roads cause leaks. Source: transcript (Girish fills after lunch; next morning drive tyres read 90 to 91 against 100). Status: supported, with limits (F14 to F16). Observed: Hubballi's detected pressure-adjustment events occur at 15:20 to 16:10 with tyres at 32 to 52 °C, Belagavi's at 06:00 to 06:50 with tyres at 22 to 28 °C, both to within about 1 PSI of the card value. At the first morning cold reading at least 12 hours later, all 69 Hubballi adjustment rows (38 distinct tyres) were 5.8 to 17.9 PSI under spec (median -10.7), and all 43 Belagavi rows (37 tyres) were within -1.9 to +2.4 PSI (median +0.3). Inferred: Hubballi adjusts to the card value hot and Belagavi cold; the data does not show who adjusts, with what gauge, or the exact procedure. Limits: cooling under this dataset's empirical 0.62 PSI per °C can explain most of the Hubballi deficit but the unexplained share ranges from about -5 to +1 PSI depending on the coefficient; the data cannot separate cooling, overnight loss, gauge differences or sensor temperature, and does not rule out a small road effect (stable between-fill loss: Hubballi -3.0 raw and -1.7 adjusted against Belagavi -1.4 and -1.3 PSI per 30 days, descriptive medians across six trucks).

H3. The Karwar 5RI failure on 11 July showed in the TPMS data before it happened. Source: transcript and Tyre Register. Test: 5RI against its twin 5RO over the preceding days and hours, looking for falling cold pressure or rising temperature relative to the twin. Status: answered by F12. There was no pressure warning on earlier days; the loss began at about 03:00 on 11 July and a twin comparison would have flagged it at 03:40.

H4. The fast reporting rate (about 18 to 20 s) means the truck is moving and the slow rate (about 2 min) means it is parked. Source: F5. Test: compare the rate with movement between GPS fixes. Status: partly supported, see F10. Resolved by F10: slow reporting goes with negligible movement, fast reporting occurs both moving and stationary (44.3% of valid intervals below 3 km/h), and the reason for switching is not established.

H5. Missing GPS on the Goa and Karwar trucks comes from ghat terrain or monsoon cloud. Source: F6. Test: where and when the fix drops out. Status: partly examined (F17). During the afternoon cooling events GPS is missing for 45% to 70% of the time on every truck, against 1% to 7% overall, including the Karwar truck while parked, where terrain cannot change. This association fits cloud or rain but does not prove it; terrain on the ghat was not tested separately.

H6. Tyre air temperatures on the Goa truck rise on the Anmod ghat descent, and the rise is larger on braked axles. Source: transcript (brake complaints on Anmod). Test: temperature by axle on the morning descent against flat running. Limit: the sensor measures air inside the tyre, not the brake drum, so this can show heat reaching the tyres but cannot prove the brakes are the source or that they are worn. Status: open.

H7. The 30 June inspection of the Karwar truck may not be reliable for the failed tyre. Source: inspection sheet. The failed tyre (DR2I, WL-T2-5RI-0242) reads 15.9 mm at all three points and its twin (DR2O) reads 12.0 mm at all three, while most other readings on the sheets vary by 0.1 to 0.4 mm across the tread. Test: check how many readings on each sheet are identical across all three points, and what 15.9 mm means for a tyre of that age. Status: open.

H8. Some twin pairs drift out of balance for days or weeks, against the spec, and the cases differ in kind. Source: C6 in Sections 4H and 4I. Gadag 5RI/5RO: a persistent outer deficit of 11 to 12 PSI from 1 to 5 June, with both tyres steady (daily minimums about 92 to 94 PSI for 5RI and 83 for 5RO); on 6 June the gap closed because 5RI fell to 5RO's level, not because 5RO was inflated; afterwards both ran at daily minimums of about 77 to 81 PSI, well below the 100 PSI cold spec, although daily minimums are not confirmed cold readings. Goa 4LI/4LO: a relative deterioration, with the median difference widening from about -4.6 PSI on 1 June to -7.2 PSI on 16 and 17 June as 4LI's daily minimum fell from 92.7 to 87.0 PSI while 4LO's went from 96.3 to 93.1; on 19 June both read about 98.5 to 99 PSI, which looks like a top-up, after which 4LI again fell behind its twin (-4.6 PSI by 25 June). I do not yet have an absolute loss rate for 4LI against the spec's 3 PSI a month, because these are not confirmed cold readings. Goa 5RI/5RO and Vijayapura 5LI/5LO show smaller persistent outer deficits. Section 5 explains the Gadag case: on 5 June at about 15:40 the Hubballi depot let air out of the truck's hot tyres to the card value, which brought 5RI down to 5RO's level (F15). On cold readings, Goa 4LI loses air several times faster than the spec allows in every interval (F16). Other causes unresolved. Status: largely answered by F15 and F16.

Detailed findings

Each finding follows the same six parts. Figures are from notebook 01_load_and_clean, Cell 2, unless stated.

F1. The June and July exports overlap by 12 hours and duplicate 2,869 records

What I checked and how. I loaded all twelve exports into one table. For each truck I compared the last timestamp in the June file with the first timestamp in the July file, then counted rows that are identical in every data column (all columns except the source file name and the Vehicle field).

Evidence. On every truck, the July export starts at about 18:00 on 30 June and the June export ends at about 06:00 on 1 July. Every row in that window appears in both files with identical values:

What it tells us. The monthly export appears to run from 18:00 on the last day of the previous month to 06:00 on the first day of the next. These are the same readings exported twice, not extra readings. Left in, the night of 30 June would be counted twice in any average, count or alarm tally.

What could change my interpretation. Nothing about the treatment. If TyreSense documents a different export window, only my explanation of why the overlap exists would change.

F2. The Goa truck's July export carries the wrong registration

What I checked and how. I compared the Vehicle value inside every file with the registration in its file name.

Evidence. Eleven files match. TyreSense_export_KA25AB4471_2026-07.csv has KA25B4471 in the Vehicle column on all 27,082 rows; the letter A is missing. The June file for the same truck reads KA25AB4471 correctly.

What it tells us. Anything that groups the data by the Vehicle column would treat the Goa truck's July data as a seventh truck and show the Goa truck as having no July data. I take truck identity from the file name instead. It also means the portal's own vehicle labels cannot be assumed correct.

Confidence. High that the file belongs to KA25AB4471: the file name says so and its data continues the June file without a break (to be confirmed when I plot the full series).

What could change my interpretation. If the July data did not continue from June (different sensor batteries or pressures at the join), the file could belong to a different receiver.

Where used. Distrust memo; HTML data-quality section.

F3. On the inspection sheets C is the left side and D the right; the data dictionary is wrong

What I checked and how. The data dictionary says C = co-driver side (right) but in the same paragraph says CF1 = front left. The transcript says C is the cleaner's side, where the helper sits, on the left. Rather than choose between them, I used the tyre serials on the inspection sheet. Westline's serials embed the TPMS position where the tyre was first fitted (WL-T1-1LO-0200 was first fitted at 1LO), so for each of the 70 inspection rows I compared the side letter in the code with the side in the serial.

Evidence. In 68 of 70 rows, C codes carry L serials and D codes carry R serials, across all five inspections and every axle. The two exceptions are the Goa truck's CF3 and DF3 on 14 July, which carry 3RO-0205 and 3LO-0204: the pair swapped on 28 June, and the Tyre Register shows exactly that swap. The sheet itself marks both rows "re-fitted 28-06". India drives on the left with the driver seated on the right, so D for driver side = right also fits.

What it tells us. C = left, D = right. The dictionary's label "co-driver side (right)" is wrong; its examples (CF1 = front left, DF1 = front right) are correct. Using the dictionary label would have put every inspection reading on the wrong side of the truck, which would reverse any left-right wear comparison.

Confidence. High. Three independent sources agree (serials, transcript, right-hand drive) and the only exceptions are explained by a recorded event.

What could change my interpretation. Evidence that serials were not assigned at the position in their name, for example tyres moved before the serial scheme began.

Where used. Distrust memo; HTML data-quality section and method note on position mapping.

F4. The June rotation was on the Goa truck, as the records show, not as Ramesh remembered

What I checked and how. Ramesh was unsure whether the left-right swap at the end of June was on the Goa or the Karwar truck, and told me to check the register. I read the Tyre Register notes and checked them against the two Goa inspections.

Evidence. The Tyre Register shows T1 (Goa) 3LO now holds WL-T1-3RO-0205 and 3RO holds WL-T1-3LO-0204, both noted "re-fitted 28-06-2026". 28 June 2026 was a Sunday, matching Ramesh's memory of the day. The 10 June inspection has 0204 on the left (CF3) and 0205 on the right (DF3); the 14 July inspection has them reversed. No Karwar tyre has a re-fit note. The only other register note is the Karwar 5RI replacement on 12 July, which also explains the only inspection serial not in the current register (WL-T2-5RI-0242, inspected on 30 June and removed on 11 July).

What it tells us. The swap was on the Goa truck, axle 3. For wear and pressure analysis I follow tyres by serial across 28 June, so tyre 0204's history continues on the right side after the swap. It also shows the transcript is reliable on the event but not on the vehicle, which is a reason to check other details in it against the records.

Confidence. High for the swap. Whether there were other unrecorded swaps I cannot tell yet; the TPMS data should show a step change in a position's pressure pattern if a tyre moved.

What could change my interpretation. A step change in the TPMS data on another truck or date, which would point to a move the register does not record.

Where used. Distrust memo (transcript is unreliable on vehicle identity); HTML fleet events; a candidate question for Ramesh about unrecorded moves.

F5. The receiver reports at two rates, about every 18 to 20 seconds and about every 2 minutes

What I checked and how. I sorted each truck's records by time and measured the gap between consecutive records.

Evidence. Across all trucks, 421,722 gaps round to about 20 seconds and 134,312 to about 2 minutes, with the median at 18 seconds. A further 2,869 gaps are zero (the F1 duplicates) and the remaining 270 are spread across other lengths. On the Gadag truck, for example, the first records after midnight on 1 June are two minutes apart.

What it tells us. The receiver almost certainly has a fast and a slow mode. My working explanation is fast while moving and slow while parked, but that is H4 and not yet shown. It matters for two reasons: averages over raw rows are weighted toward driving time, and if the slow rate does mean parked, it gives me a way to find cold readings.

Confidence. High for the two rates. Low for what they mean until H4 is tested.

What could change my interpretation. If the rate does not follow movement, it may follow something else, such as ignition state or a pressure-change trigger.

Where used. HTML method section; possibly the definition of cold readings.

F6. GPS fixes are missing far more often on the Goa and Karwar trucks

What I checked and how. Share of records per file with no latitude.

Evidence. Goa truck: 15.9% (June) and 18.8% (July). Karwar truck: 13.2% and 16.4%. The other four trucks: 0.8% to 4.3%.

What it tells us. The two trucks that run down the Western Ghats to the coast lose GPS several times more often. Terrain, monsoon cloud or the receiver installation could explain it (H5). Until then, distances or locations computed from GPS on these two trucks will be incomplete, which matters when I compare the kilometre log with GPS distance.

Confidence. High for the figures. Low for the cause.

What could change my interpretation. If the gaps fall on the ghat sections, terrain is the likely cause; if they are spread through the day, depot parking or the receiver is more likely.

Where used. Distrust memo (GPS incompleteness); HTML data-quality section.

F7. The exports pass the basic integrity checks

What I checked and how. Total rows, empty slots, duplicates within each file, and gaps longer than one hour.

Evidence. 559,179 rows in total. No duplicate rows inside any single file. No gap over one hour on any truck between 1 June and 15 July. The 24 columns for axles 6 and 7 contain no values at all, as the data dictionary says.

What it tells us. Apart from F1 and F2, the exports are complete at the receiver level: no truck goes dark for an hour or more. That alone does not show that every tyre sensor was reporting, because the receiver can keep sending records while one sensor is silent. Sensor-level completeness is checked separately in F8.

Confidence. High.

What could change my interpretation. Sensor-level gaps or impossible values, which are the next checks.

Where used. HTML data-quality section (one line).

F8. Every sensor reading is physically plausible; four sensors drop packets about three times as often as the rest

What I checked and how. In Section 3 I removed the F1 duplicates (559,179 records down to 556,310), reshaped the data to one row per truck, time and tyre (7,788,340 rows), and then checked every reading for impossible values, missing packets per sensor and the longest silence per tyre.

Evidence. No reading has pressure at or below zero or above 160 PSI, temperature below 0 °C or above 100 °C, battery outside 0 to 100%, or pressure without a temperature. Across all trucks and positions, temperatures run from 18.9 °C to 82.9 °C. Most sensors miss 1.1 to 1.2% of records. Four miss more: Gadag 5RO (3.5%), Goa 4LI (3.5%), Karwar 3LO (3.5%) and Shivamogga 2RO (2.9%); Karwar 5RI misses 2.1%, which is the failure day (F9). Apart from Karwar 5RI, no tyre is silent for longer than about 12 minutes at a stretch.

What it tells us. I can use the pressure and temperature values as recorded, without filtering out bad readings. A 1% packet loss is normal radio dropout. The four higher-loss sensors are still reporting most of the time, so they do not hide events, but they may be weaker transmitters or have lower batteries.

Confidence. High for the values being plausible. Low for why four sensors drop more packets.

What could change my interpretation. If the higher-loss sensors also have the lowest batteries, or their dropouts cluster at particular times or places, that would point to a failing sensor rather than random radio loss.

Where used. HTML data-quality section; possibly a line in the distrust memo on sensor reliability.

F9. The Karwar 5RI tyre read below 70 PSI from 09:03 until its last reading at 14:19 on 11 July

What I checked and how. I listed every tyre in the fleet that ever read below 70 PSI (30% under the drive-axle spec of 100), with the first and last time and the lowest reading, and I looked for the longest silence on any sensor.

Evidence. Only one tyre in six trucks and six weeks ever went below 70 PSI: Karwar truck (KA25AC1182) position 5RI, the inner right tyre on axle 5. It was below 70 PSI from 09:03:50 to 14:19:41 on 11 July, 1,077 readings, falling to 13.9 PSI. F10. Slow reporting goes with negligible movement; fast reporting occurs both moving and stationary

What I checked and how. Section 3C (V6 revised after a second review). The data is explicitly sorted by truck and time. Intervals where either record has no fix get no speed and are counted separately, not as stationary. Evidence. Fast mode: 421,731 intervals, 383,975 with a valid speed and 37,756 without. F13. A two-level twin-difference warning is a candidate worth testing; at 10 and 20 PSI the historical alert burden is low, but a reset band is needed

What I checked and how. Section 4I, revised C6. For all 24 twin pairs (six trucks, axles 4 and 5, left and right) I built a complete 10-minute timeline with no bins dropped. Each bin holds the median inner-minus-outer difference over timestamps where both tyres reported; bins with no paired reading are "no data". A bin qualifies when the difference passes the threshold in one direction (inner deficit or outer deficit, counted separately). An episode is at least two consecutive qualifying bins (20 minutes); one "no data" bin inside a run does not break it, and anything else does. I report qualifying bins and episodes separately, at 5, 10 and 20 PSI. These are historical alert episodes, not confirmed false alarms: some reflect real inflation problems.

Evidence. 5 PSI: 118 inner-deficit episodes (1,796 bins) and 149 outer-deficit episodes (2,632 bins), across six pairs plus Karwar; the largest are Goa 4LI/4LO inner deficit (115), Goa 5RI/5RO outer deficit (61), Gadag 5RI/5RO outer deficit (55) and Vijayapura 5LI/5LO outer deficit (33). 10 PSI: 21 episodes; 20 are Gadag 5RI/5RO outer deficit between 1 and 5 June, and one is Karwar 5RI on 11 July (03:40 to 14:20). 20 PSI: one episode, Karwar 5RI from 06:10. What it tells us. Confidence. High for the counts under the stated rules. Low for how the rule would perform on other failures: there is one failure to test against, six weeks of data, and no coverage of the single tyres on axles 1 to 3, which have no twin.

What could change my interpretation. More months of data and more failures; a different minimum duration or reset band; failures on single axles, which would need a different comparison (left against right, or against the tyre's own history).
